A Snapshot of Female-headed Establishments in India: Key Insights from the Annual Survey of Unincorporated Sector Enterprises 2025

Gender-disaggregated analysis of growth, sectors, states, registration and access to credit.

By Manya Aggarwal, Phalasha Nagpal and Gaurav Kumar Sinha

What share of establishments are female-headed, what does the growth look like, and has the growth led to hiring?
Insights 1.1–1.4

Section 1 · Growth & Structure

Udaiti’s take

Women own 28.6% of India’s unincorporated non-agricultural establishments, but growth in numbers has not translated into scale. Most remain micro or subsistence-driven, with only about 3% as hired-worker establishments (HWEs), compared with about 17% of male-headed establishments. Some of this growth may be necessity-driven, reflecting limited wage employment opportunities, household income shocks and reliance on home-based work.

Within this base, Growth-Oriented Women Enterprises (GoWEs), units demonstrating proven operating scale, commercial ambition, and viability, remain underserved due to gender inequities in access to finance, markets and growth support. Addressing these barriers can enable GoWEs to scale, driving greater investment, productivity and employment, particularly for women.

↓ The evidence for this take follows in Insights 1.1 to 1.4.

Insight 1.1

Female-headed establishments make up 28.6% of India’s proprietary establishments, yet few of them are hired-worker establishments, a pattern consistent across all three broad industries

Within India’s 74.9 million proprietary establishments, 21.4 million are female-headed. Only 3.0% of these are hired-worker establishments (HWEs), against 16.8% of male-headed establishments. The HWE gap is widest in manufacturing, even though female-headed establishments outnumber male-headed ones there. Although female-headed establishments are growing, 97.0% remain own-account establishments (OAEs).
1.1.1
All industries | By gender

28.6% of proprietary establishments are female-headed

In 2025, 21.4 million proprietary establishments (28.6%) were female-headed, against 53.5 million male-headed.

For every five male-headed establishments, there are about two female-headed establishments.

1.1.2
All industries | Hiring

Only 3.0% are female-headed hired-worker establishments

Only 634.2 thousand female-headed establishments (3.0%) are HWEs, against 9.0 million (16.8%) male-headed establishments.

Male-headed establishments are 5.6 times as likely to employ hired workers as female-headed establishments.

1.1.3
Manufacturing | By gender

Female-headed establishments outnumber male-headed ones, yet show the widest HWE gap of any industry

Female-headed manufacturing establishments outnumber male-headed ones (12.9 million vs. 8.4 million), accounting for three in every five establishments. However, only 1.2% of female-headed establishments are HWEs, compared to 25.0% of male-headed establishments.

Male-headed establishments are 20.8 times as likely to employ hired workers as female-headed establishments, marking the widest gender gap across the three industries. The gap is 4.2 times in trade and about twice in other services.

Insight 1.2

Female-headed establishments accounted for 45% of the net increase between 2022–23 and 2025

Female-headed establishments made up only 24.4% of proprietary establishments in 2022–23, but accounted for 4.5 million (45%) of the 10.0 million net increase by 2025, comparing only activities covered in both rounds. Growth among newer establishments (started in the last three years) was similarly concentrated in own-account establishments.
1.2.1
Growth since 2022–23

45% of the net increase in proprietary establishments was female-headed

Comparing only activities covered in both survey rounds, the net addition in proprietary establishments between 2022–23 and 2025 was 10.0 million, of which 4.5 million (45%) were female-headed.

OAEs drove nearly all of this growth: 97% of the female-headed increase, against 89% for male-headed establishments.

1.2.2
New establishments

Female-headed establishments are a growing share of new establishments

Looking at establishments with up to three years of operation, 36.1% (5.1 million) are female-headed, up from 30.0% in 2022–23.

However, the vast majority (96.5%) of them are OAEs (4.9 million).

1.2.3
New female-headed establishments by sector

84.2% of new female-headed establishments are in just six sectors

More than half of female-headed establishments operating for up to three years are in manufacturing, led by wearing apparel (1.9 million). The six leading sectors are wearing apparel, retail trade, other personal service activities*, education*, textiles and tobacco products. Section 2 looks at these sectors in detail.

Insight 1.3

Female-headed hired-worker establishments generate 12.2 times as much gross value added (GVA) per establishment as female-headed own-account establishments

Female-headed HWEs average ₹8.3 lakh in GVA, 12.2 times the ₹68.3 thousand of female-headed OAEs and close to the ₹8.8 lakh of male-headed HWEs. Among male-headed establishments, the ratio is 4.9 times.
1.3.1
As own-account

Female-headed OAEs generate less than half the average GVA of male-headed OAEs

Female-headed OAEs generate an average annual GVA of ₹68.3 thousand, compared to ₹1.8 lakh for male-headed ones. The gap is widest in manufacturing, at ₹47.9 thousand versus ₹1.7 lakh per establishment.

1.3.2
With workers

Female-headed establishments nearly match male-headed ones in average GVA when they hire workers

Female-headed HWEs generate an average annual GVA of ₹8.3 lakh, compared to ₹8.8 lakh for male-headed ones. In trade, female-headed HWEs marginally outperform male-headed ones, at ₹8.5 lakh versus ₹8.4 lakh per establishment.

1.3.3
The gap

Female-headed HWEs generate 7–16 times as much GVA as female-headed OAEs, a larger disparity than among male-headed establishments

Across industries, female-headed HWEs generate 12.2 times the average GVA of female-headed OAEs, compared to 4.9 times for male-headed establishments. The gap is widest in manufacturing (16.3 times for female-headed vs. 5.8 times for male-headed) and narrowest in trade (7.1 times for female-headed vs. 4.4 times for male-headed).

Insight 1.4

Female-headed establishments hire fewer workers, but a higher proportion of those hired are women.

Female-headed establishments employ 26.3 million workers, of whom 6.8% (1.8 million) are hired, against 26.4% in male-headed establishments. Yet women account for 57.5% of their hired workforce (up from 52.1% in 2022–23), compared to just 16.2% in male-headed establishments. Since 2022–23, eight in ten additional hired workers in female-headed establishments have been women. Across the broader enterprise landscape, hired workers grew by only 10.0%, lagging behind the expansion of establishments (21.6%) and total workers (17.2%).
1.4.1
Establishments, workers and hired workers

Workers grew more slowly than establishments, and hired workers slower still

India’s 74.9 million proprietary establishments employ 119.8 million workers, of whom only 26.5 million are hired workers. Since 2022–23, establishments grew by 21.6%, workers by 17.2% and hired workers by only 10.0%.

1.4.2
By gender

In female-headed establishments, 1 in 15 workers is hired, compared to 1 in 4 in male-headed establishments

Female-headed establishments account for 26.3 million workers, including 1.8 million hired workers (6.8%). In comparison, 26.4% of total workers in male-headed establishments are hired (24.7 million out of 93.5 million).

Which sectors offer the greatest opportunities for growth?

Section 2 · Sectors

Udaiti’s take

Female-headed establishments are concentrated in manufacturing, followed by other services and trade. Within each, they cluster where hiring is limited: traditionally feminised sectors such as wearing apparel, textiles and food products in manufacturing, retail rather than wholesale in trade, and own-account other personal service activities in services. Higher hiring in wholesale trade, human health activities, accommodation, and food and beverage service activities signals greater potential for employment generation.

Market-led approaches can help scale female-headed establishments where a market constraint is binding. In manufacturing, cluster development, enterprise aggregation and stronger value-chain linkages can expand hiring, open access to larger markets and enable a move into higher-value activities. In trade, stronger buyer-supplier linkages and access to working capital can support entry into wholesale. In services, digital market access and targeted business support can help enterprises reach wider markets and scale.

↓ The evidence for this take follows in Insights 2.1 to 2.4.

Insight 2.1 · All industries

The gender gap in the share of HWEs barely narrowed between 2022–23 and 2025: by 0.8 percentage points, or 0.6 on a like-for-like basis

HWEs grew in number for both genders, but more slowly than establishments overall, so the HWE share fell for both female-headed (3.4% to 3.0%) and male-headed establishments (18.0% to 16.8%). The gender gap narrowed from 14.6 to 13.8 percentage points only because the male-headed share fell faster.The female-headed HWE share fell most in other services (10.2% to 6.6%), dipped in manufacturing (1.4% to 1.2%) and held steady in trade (4.3%). The fall in other services is largely a coverage effect: the newly covered activities were predominantly OAEs. On a like-for-like basis, that share rose from 10.7% to 11.1%, and the overall female-headed HWE share was virtually flat (3.4% to 3.3%).

Insight 2.2 · Manufacturing

Six in ten female-headed establishments are in manufacturing, concentrated primarily in three sectors

Manufacturing accounts for 60.5% of female-headed proprietary establishments, yet female-headed HWEs increased by only 16.1 thousand between 2022–23 and 2025, with the HWE share declining from 1.4% to 1.2%. The female-headed HWE share fell in 8 of 15 manufacturing sectors, rose in 5 and was unchanged in 2.
2.2.1
Manufacturing

Female-headed establishments accounted for nine in ten new manufacturing establishments

Between 2022–23 and 2025, female-headed establishments accounted for 3.2 million of the 3.6 million net additions in manufacturing. Three sectors contributed 86.0% of these additions: wearing apparel (+2.0 million), textiles (+486.7 thousand) and tobacco products (+264.5 thousand).

Insight 2.3 · Trade

About one in seven female-headed establishments is in trade, predominantly in retail trade

Trade holds 15.2% of female-headed establishments. Retail trade accounts for 98.1% of them, but only 3.5% of female-headed retail trade establishments are HWEs. By contrast, wholesale trade holds only 1.7%, yet 37.1% of female-headed wholesale trade establishments are HWEs. Female-headed establishments are concentrated where trade hires the least and nearly absent where it hires the most.
2.3.1
Trade

465.0 thousand female-headed trade establishments were added, almost all in retail trade

Retail trade contributed 451.4 thousand (97.1%) of them. Female-headed establishments made up less than a third of all new trade establishments (465.0 thousand of 1.6 million).

Insight 2.4 · Other services

About one in four female-headed establishments is in other services, the fastest-growing and most diverse industry

Other services holds 24.3% of India’s female-headed establishments, up from 16.1% in 2022–23. Among service sectors, other personal service activities added the most, almost all as OAEs. The sectors where female-headed HWEs are most common, such as accommodation and human health activities, hold only a small share of female-headed establishments.
2.4.1
Other services

Other personal service activities alone added 2.0 million female-headed establishments

Other services added 2.8 million female-headed establishments between 2022–23 and 2025, out of 8.1 million in total, with 71.4% (2.0 million) coming from other personal service activities; education added another 287.1 thousand and food and beverage service activities 132.8 thousand. This increase reflects both actual establishment growth and expanded survey coverage, particularly in other personal service activities, education and real estate activities.

Section 3: States
How do these opportunities and constraints vary across states?

Section 3 · States

Udaiti’s take

The states with the most female-headed establishments are not necessarily the ones where they have hired workers. These differences are shaped by industrial and sectoral composition, labour supply and demand, demographics, gender norms, socioeconomic conditions, infrastructure and private-sector participation, so the opportunities and constraints facing female-headed establishments vary from state to state. This calls for context-specific, gender-responsive strategies, with state governments playing a central role in anchoring interventions, driving convergence and enabling impact at scale.

↓ The evidence for this take follows in Insights 3.1 and 3.2.

Insight 3.1 · Establishments by state

The states with the most female-headed own-account establishments differ significantly from the states with female-headed hired-worker establishments providing employment

Uttar Pradesh leads on total proprietary establishments, West Bengal on female-headed establishments, and Maharashtra, Tamil Nadu and Kerala on female-headed HWEs.
3.1.1
Female-headed establishments

West Bengal has the most female-headed establishments

The largest female-headed base is in West Bengal: 3.6 million establishments, or 16.8% of India’s total, ahead of Uttar Pradesh (2.2 million) and Maharashtra (1.9 million).

Insight 3.2 · HWE share by state

More female-headed establishments do not necessarily ensure more hired-worker establishments and greater employment generation

West Bengal has the largest female-headed base, yet only 0.9% of these establishments are HWEs. Tamil Nadu, Kerala, Maharashtra, Haryana and Assam combine a large base with an above-median HWE share. The size of a state’s female-headed base says little about its share of female-headed HWEs.
3.2.1
Female-headed base and HWE share

A larger base of female-headed establishments does not necessarily translate into a higher female-headed HWE share

States show considerable variation in the share of female-headed hired-worker establishments.

These differences may reflect variations in socioeconomic conditions, entrepreneurial networks, access to credit and markets, institutional support, and ease of doing business. The chart highlights these patterns but does not establish which factors explain them.

Section 4: Registration & Credit
But how do registration and loan sizes differ between male- and female-headed establishments?

Section 4 · Registration & Credit

Udaiti’s take

In most worker-size categories, the average institutional loan held by female-headed establishments remains lower than that held by male-headed establishments.

Evidence shows that key barriers are both demand- and supply-side and include limited awareness and information gaps, lack of collateral and guarantors, inadequate commercial evidence for accessing larger-ticket loans, and informality. Formal credit products and processes also often fail to adequately address the specific needs of female-headed establishments.

Limited access to formal credit remains a critical constraint to the growth and sustainability of female-headed establishments. With appropriate support, GoWEs can progressively access larger loans, expand operations, and generate additional employment opportunities, particularly for women, thereby enhancing their economic contribution.

↓ The evidence for this take follows in Insights 4.1 and 4.2.

Insight 4.1 · Who is registered

Female-headed OAEs have low registration rates across industries. The gender gap is smaller among hired-worker establishments, where female-headed establishments lead in trade and other services

Registration rises with establishment size, with female-headed HWEs in the 15+ worker-size category leading across all three industries. Smaller female-headed manufacturing HWEs, where registration rates remain below 50%, need to be prioritised.
4.1.1
Manufacturing

Only 3.7% of female-headed OAEs are registered, compared to 23.3% of male-headed OAEs

Manufacturing has the widest gender gap in OAE registration, and 98.8% of female-headed manufacturing establishments are OAEs. Among HWEs, female-headed HWEs have higher registration rates than male-headed HWEs in the 10–14 worker-size category and above.

4.1.2
Trade

Female-headed OAEs trail male-headed OAEs on registration, while the pattern reverses for HWEs

In trade, 23.7% of female-headed OAEs are registered, against 36.5% of male-headed OAEs. Among HWEs, registration rates are similar for both genders in the 1–2 worker-size category, but higher for female-headed HWEs in every other worker-size category, with the widest gap in the 6–9 worker-size category.

4.1.3
Other services

Female-headed OAEs have lower registration rates than male-headed OAEs, but the pattern reverses for HWEs in four of the five worker-size categories

Only 8.5% of female-headed OAEs in other services are registered, compared to 25.7% of male-headed OAEs. Among HWEs, female-headed HWEs have higher registration rates than male-headed HWEs in four of the five worker-size categories, the exception being the 6–9 worker-size category.

Insight 4.2 · Average loan size

Female-headed OAEs hold substantially smaller institutional loans than male-headed OAEs, while the gap among HWEs varies by industry

The gap in institutional loan amounts is particularly pronounced in the 15+ worker-size category. Here, male-headed establishments hold average institutional loans 1.7 times as large as female-headed establishments in manufacturing, and over 3 times as large in other services. Trade shows the opposite pattern, with female-headed establishments in the same worker-size category holding nearly twice the average loan amount of male-headed establishments, though this figure rests on only 30 sampled establishments and should be read with care.
4.2.1
Manufacturing

Male-headed establishments hold larger average loans than female-headed establishments in every worker-size category except 3–5 workers

Among OAEs with institutional loans, female-headed establishments hold an average of ₹50.1 thousand, compared to ₹1.6 lakh for male-headed establishments. Among HWEs, female-headed establishments hold larger average loans only in the 3–5 worker-size category. The widest gender gap is observed in the 15+ worker-size category.

Trade

Female-headed OAEs hold smaller institutional loans than male-headed OAEs, while the pattern varies across worker-size categories among HWEs

Among OAEs with institutional loans, female-headed establishments hold an average of ₹1.1 lakh, compared to ₹2.9 lakh for male-headed establishments. In contrast, female-headed HWEs hold larger average loans than male-headed HWEs in three of the five worker-size categories.

Other services

Institutional loan sizes are broadly similar across genders for HWEs, except in the 15+ worker-size category

What interventions can address these barriers and enable female-headed establishments to scale?

Conclusion

Udaiti’s take

The constraint on women’s enterprise in India lies less in the number of establishments than in their ability to scale. The large existing base, particularly in manufacturing, offers significant job-creation potential. Yet, despite well-documented gender barriers, existing support remains fragmented and does not adequately address women’s specific needs and constraints. Addressing these gaps requires targeted, bundled and gender-responsive interventions, grounded in evidence and focused on measurable outcomes, such as turnover growth and job creation.

Given the variation in outcomes across states, one approach is to anchor interventions, tailored to local contexts, in state governments. Convening departments and ecosystem partners to deliver coordinated support can provide women entrepreneurs with integrated access to the resources needed to scale.

A second approach is to anchor interventions in a clearly defined market constraint and a committed market actor, testing gender-responsive solutions and scaling what works. Our experience shows that early government engagement can enable successful models to scale.
